## Week 1 · Act like an owner, not a role

During the first week, we formed our team and decided who would be responsible for what. I took the Delivery role, which meant keeping track of deadlines and making sure we submitted our work on time.

I wrote three problem statements about issues that I thought were worth investigating. I also helped update our README with the team information and signed the Team Contract.

Even though my main role was Delivery, I didn't want to be involved only when something needed to be submitted. I wanted to contribute to the actual project decisions too, especially while we were choosing which problem to work on.

*My contributions:* [Problem Pool](../../00-foundation/problem-pool.md), [Team Contract](../../00-foundation/team-contract.md), [README](../../README.md)

## Week 2 · Evidence over opinion

This week, we started working on the campus laundry problem. Each of us had a slightly different idea about what the biggest issue was. My opinion was that students often leave their finished laundry inside washing machines, making other students wait unnecessarily.

I added my personal hypothesis to our shared Problem Hypothesis file. Having everyone's ideas in one place made it easier to compare them and choose what we wanted to investigate first.

I also created my personal GitHub folder and the files we would need for our individual assignments.

The main thing I understood was that having an opinion about a problem wasn't enough. We needed interviews to find out whether students actually experienced what we were describing.

*My contributions:* [Problem Hypothesis](../../01-discovery/problem-hypothesis.md), [Personal Folder](./), [DECISIONS.md](../../DECISIONS.md)

## Week 3 · Kill your favorite idea

This week, I worked on our interview questions, including the warm-up and core questions. I wanted the questions to be understandable and give students a chance to describe their own experiences.

I also took part in conducting and documenting interviews. We were originally focused on unattended laundry, but students talked about several other difficulties as well, including broken machines, unreliable dryers, and having to check machine availability repeatedly.

I realized that our original idea could be part of the problem without being the only problem. We couldn't just focus on the answers that supported our hypothesis and ignore everything else.

Working on the interviews helped me understand why we should keep asking about real experiences instead of immediately suggesting possible solutions.

*My contributions:* [Interview Script v1](../../01-discovery/interview-script-v1.md), [Interview Logs](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/real/), [Problem Hypothesis](../../01-discovery/problem-hypothesis.md)

## Week 4 · The prototype is the conversation

This week, I worked on several important parts of our Milestone 1. I added interview notes, updated our ICP, worked on the Problem Statement, and revised DECISIONS.md.

I also helped document the interviews. What stood out from the findings was that students had different experiences with the same laundry facilities. Some were still satisfied despite the problems, while others had much more serious difficulties.

When working on the Problem Statement, I had to make sure we were describing real problems rather than just writing what we thought sounded right. I also learned how important it was to connect each claim to the interview that supported it.

Although my main role is Delivery, I spent time working on Discovery and the actual content of our project. We were preparing the Milestone files while new interviews were still being completed, so we could update the evidence instead of leaving everything until the last moment.

We haven't built our prototype yet, but the interviews have given us a clearer idea of what we need to investigate before building anything.
